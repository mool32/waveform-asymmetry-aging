# Asymmetric Perception Cycles as a Universal Principle of Living Systems

## A Research Framework: From Bicycle Physics to Cross-Scale Biomarkers

---

## 1. Origin of the Idea

### 1.1 The Bicycle Observation

A circular cycling route through hilly terrain has zero net elevation change — yet one direction is significantly easier than the other. The direction with short steep climbs followed by long gentle descents is easier than the reverse (long gentle climbs followed by short steep descents).

From the standpoint of conservative (non-living) physics, this should not matter: for an ideal body in vacuum, work around a closed loop equals zero. The asymmetry arises from two factors that are fundamentally biological:

**Factor 1: Dissipation under stability constraints.** On steep descents, the cyclist must brake — not because the energy is unwanted, but because high speed exceeds the organism's zone of stability. Braking irreversibly converts kinetic energy to heat. On gentle descents, speed stays within the stability zone and no braking is needed — the gravitational energy is fully utilized. Thus, the route requiring less braking (steep up, gentle down) is thermodynamically cheaper.

**Factor 2: Nonlinear recovery dynamics.** Living systems have rate-dependent efficiency. Short intense efforts (anaerobic burst on a steep climb) followed by long recovery periods (gentle descent) are metabolically cheaper than prolonged moderate effort (long climb) with no recovery window.

**The key insight:** The asymmetry arises not from the engine (muscles) but from the cost of maintaining stability. The organism actively spends energy to remain within its viability zone. A rock on a cart has no viability zone — it "doesn't care" about speed. A living system does care, and the cost of that caring creates the asymmetry.

### 1.2 Generalization

This observation generalizes into a principle:

> **For any living system, the temporal pattern of perturbations matters as much as their total magnitude. A sequence of brief, intense excitations followed by long recovery periods is energetically and informationally cheaper than the reverse — because each recovery period allows the system to restore its sensitivity within its zone of viability, while rapid perturbations without recovery force costly regulatory "braking" or lead to system failure.**

## 2. The Asymmetric Perception Cycle

### 2.1 Core Claim

Every act of biological perception — from molecular receptor binding to conscious awareness — follows an asymmetric cycle:

**Excitation** (fast, cheap) → **Adaptation** → **Recovery of sensitivity** (slow, expensive)

- Excitation is fast because it utilizes pre-stored energy (membrane potential, phosphocreatine, vesicular stores, conformational strain in proteins).
- Recovery is slow because it requires active resynthesis, recycling, and transport (SERCA pumps, receptor recycling, neurotransmitter reuptake, chromatin remodeling).

This asymmetry is not a defect — it is a fundamental design principle. It creates:

- **Memory**: the system retains a trace of stimulation during the slow recovery phase.
- **Noise filtering**: brief noise doesn't trigger full cycles; only sustained or strong signals do.
- **Directionality**: the system has a preferred temporal pattern of stimulation (pulsatile > tonic).

### 2.2 The Universality of Pulsatile Signaling

If our framework is correct, pulsatile (asymmetric cyclic) signaling should be the rule in biology, and tonic signaling should be the exception or a pathological state. This is confirmed across all scales:

| Level | Pulsatile example | Tonic = pathological |
|-------|-------------------|----------------------|
| Gene expression | Transcriptional bursts | Constitutive expression (oncogenes) |
| Intracellular | Ca²⁺ oscillations, NF-κB oscillations, p53 pulses | Sustained Ca²⁺ → apoptosis |
| Endocrine | GnRH pulses (90 min), insulin pulses (5-10 min), cortisol ultradian pulses | Tonic GnRH → gonadotropin suppression; chronic cortisol → Cushing's |
| Neural | Action potentials (all-or-nothing spikes) | Depolarization block (epilepsy) |
| Cardiac | Heart rhythm with HRV | Loss of HRV → cardiac death predictor |
| Immune | Acute inflammation → resolution | Chronic inflammation → autoimmunity |
| Circadian | Sleep-wake cycling | Disrupted circadian rhythm → metabolic syndrome |

### 2.3 The Neuron as the Absolute Case

The action potential represents the maximal possible asymmetry of the perception cycle:

1. **All-or-nothing**: binary, no gradation — maximum contrast between excitation and rest.
2. **Extreme temporal asymmetry**: depolarization ~1 ms, refractory period ~3-5 ms (ratio ≥ 3:1).
3. **Built-in braking**: the refractory period is not a side effect but an engineered stability constraint (analogous to the cyclist's brakes), preventing runaway excitation.
4. **Signal regeneration**: the spike regenerates at each membrane segment, solving the problem of long-distance communication without signal degradation.

The neuron evolved this extreme form because neural systems operate in environments where the speed of relevant events exceeds chemical diffusion rates. The action potential is evolution's answer to fast, dangerous environments — and it achieves both speed (steep climb) and stability (mandatory gentle descent) simultaneously.

## 3. Nested Cycles Across Scales

### 3.1 Hierarchy of Perception Cycles

The organism is not a single perception cycle but a hierarchy of nested cycles operating at different timescales:

| Scale | Timescale | Example cycle | Excitation | Recovery |
|-------|-----------|--------------|------------|----------|
| Molecular | ns–µs | Ion channel gating | Activation: ~0.1 ms | Inactivation: ~1-10 ms |
| Subcellular | ms–s | Ca²⁺ transient | IP3-release: ~100 ms | SERCA pump: ~1-5 s |
| Cellular | s–min | Action potential burst | Spike train: ~100 ms | Inter-burst interval: ~1-10 s |
| Tissue | min–h | Inflammatory wave | Neutrophil infiltration: hours | Resolution: days |
| Organ | h–days | Cardiac cycle (HRV) | Sympathetic acceleration: seconds | Parasympathetic recovery: seconds-minutes |
| Organism | days–months | Immune response | Acute phase: days | Resolution: weeks |
| Developmental | months–years | Growth spurt | Rapid growth: months | Consolidation: years |

### 3.2 Inter-Scale Coupling Rules

**Rule 1: The fast level is a "stimulus" for the slow level; the slow level is a "context" for the fast level.**

- Ca²⁺ oscillations (seconds) → via frequency coding → gene expression changes (hours)
- Gene expression → determines which Ca²⁺ channels are present → constrains which fast cycles are possible

**Rule 2: The "descent" (recovery phase) of each upper level is filled with "ascents" (excitation cycles) of the lower level.**

When the cyclist rests on a gentle descent, the muscle cells are actively resynthesizing ATP (fast cycles), and mitochondria within them run electron transport chains (even faster cycles). Every "rest" at one scale is "work" at the scale below.

**Rule 3: Healthy coupling requires frequency separation.**

Information streams at different scales must not interfere. This requires that each level operates at a characteristic frequency sufficiently separated from neighboring levels. When this separation breaks down — disease.

### 3.3 Pathology as Coupling Failure

**Upward breach (fast level invades slow):**
- Epilepsy: ms-scale neuronal spikes synchronize and impose their rhythm on the entire cortex, destroying slower cognitive cycles.
- Cancer: hourly cell-division cycle escapes tissue-level control (weekly-monthly scale).
- Cytokine storm: minute-scale cellular signaling erupts to systemic (day) scale without adequate slow braking.

**Downward breach (slow level suppresses fast):**
- Chronic stress: cortisol (circadian cycle) becomes tonic, suppressing fast immune response cycles.
- Insulin resistance: chronic hyperinsulinemia (tonic signal) desensitizes fast insulin receptor cycling.
- Coma: slow-wave EEG dominates, fast cortical perception cycles cease.

**Decoupling (levels lose coordination):**
- Aging: cross-system coherence degrades before individual systems fail.
- Neurodegeneration: loss of cortico-cardiac coupling precedes cognitive decline.

## 4. Applications to Major Biological Phenomena

### 4.1 Embryogenesis

Embryogenesis is a cascade of perception cycles where each cycle produces a cell that perceives differently. Rapid inductive signal (morphogen, Wnt, Shh) → long stabilization period (chromatin remodeling, establishment of feedback loops). The "steep climb → gentle descent" pattern ensures each new state is consolidated before the next induction. Developmental stages are fixed precisely because each stage requires a specific recovery time.

### 4.2 Aging

Aging is the degradation of the perception cycle itself, at three levels simultaneously:

1. **Excitation weakens**: receptors desensitize chronically, signaling cascades become noisy (somatic mutations, epigenetic drift).
2. **Recovery becomes slower and costlier**: proteasome efficiency declines, chaperones are overloaded, mitochondrial ATP production falls.
3. **Cross-scale coupling degrades**: the coherence between organ-level cycles (brain, heart, gut, lungs) decreases before any individual cycle fails catastrophically.

Additionally: loss of novel stimulation → cycles don't launch → recovery machinery atrophies from disuse. This connects to the concept of aging as loss of goal-directedness (целеполагание).

### 4.3 Cancer

Cancer is a cell that has lost the "descent" phase of its perception cycle. Constitutive activation of signaling (e.g., mutant RAS — permanent "on") eliminates the refractory period. The cell enters tonic self-stimulation, losing sensitivity to neighbors (contact inhibition lost), to apoptotic signals (p53 mutated — the "brake" is removed), and to immune signals. Structurally identical to addiction at the neural level: broken refractory period → runaway excitation → loss of perception.

### 4.4 Disease Classification

Through this framework, diseases divide into two classes:

- **Class 1: "Stuck on the climb"** — system excited, cannot recover. Chronic inflammation, cytokine storm, autoimmunity, chronic stress, epilepsy, mania.
- **Class 2: "Cannot climb"** — system lost excitability. Depression, immunodeficiency, Type 2 diabetes (insulin resistance), coma.

Class 2 is often the long-term consequence of Class 1: chronic tonic stimulation → desensitization → loss of response.

### 4.5 Trauma

Trauma is instantaneous breach of the viability zone — "brakes failed on a steep descent." Recovery = re-embryogenesis under less controlled conditions, producing scar tissue (coarse asymmetric cycling) instead of original architecture (fine-tuned asymmetric cycling). PTSD follows the same pattern at the neural level: the traumatic circuit never completed its recovery phase and remains chronically activated.

## 5. Formalization

### 5.1 Asymmetry Index A(τ)

For any biological oscillatory signal decomposed into timescales τ (via wavelets or bandpass filtering), at each scale we measure the rise time t_rise and fall time t_fall of each oscillatory cycle. The asymmetry index is:

$$A(\tau) = \frac{t_{fall} - t_{rise}}{t_{fall} + t_{rise}}$$

- A > 0: "fast up, slow down" — the healthy pattern
- A = 0: symmetric oscillation
- A < 0: "slow up, fast down" — potentially pathological

This is measured for each timescale τ, yielding a profile A(τ) — the asymmetry spectrum.

### 5.2 Cross-System Coherence

For two simultaneously recorded signals from different organ systems (e.g., EEG and HRV), compute A_EEG(τ) and A_HRV(τ). Their correlation:

$$\rho_{cross} = \text{corr}(A_{EEG}(\tau), A_{HRV}(\tau))$$

measures the degree to which neural and cardiac perception cycles are coupled. This is the proposed novel biomarker.

### 5.3 Inter-Scale Information Flow

Using transfer entropy (TE) between wavelet scales:

- TE(τ_fast → τ_slow): ascending flow — "stimulus from below"
- TE(τ_slow → τ_fast): descending flow — "context from above"

Net directional flow: ΔTE = TE(fast→slow) − TE(slow→fast)

In a healthy system, ascending and descending flows should be approximately balanced. Imbalance indicates pathological coupling.

### 5.4 Relation to Spectral Exponent β

The spectral exponent β (from 1/f^β power spectrum) reflects the balance of contributions from all timescales. β ≈ 1 (1/f noise, "pink noise") means no privileged scale — all levels contribute equally. Through our framework:

- β ≈ 1 is the signature of properly coupled asymmetric cycles across all scales.
- β too high (brownian noise): slow cycles dominate, fast cycles suppressed → coma-like.
- β too low (white noise): hierarchy destroyed, all cycles equally weighted → seizure-like.

The novel contribution: β alone doesn't capture directionality. A(τ) does. Two signals can have the same β but different A(τ) profiles. The asymmetry spectrum is a richer descriptor than the spectral exponent alone.

## 6. Specific, Testable Predictions

### Prediction 1: A(τ) > 0 at all biologically relevant timescales

The "fast up, slow down" asymmetry is a universal signature of living systems. This should hold from ion channel gating (µs) to circadian rhythms (hours). Can be verified from published data on oscillatory waveforms across all scales.

### Prediction 2: A(τ) decreases with age

Aging = flattening of the perception cycle. Excitation becomes weaker/slower, recovery doesn't proportionally speed up. Quantitatively: A(τ) in young > A(τ) in old, at matching timescales. Testable on LEMON dataset (age range 20-80, simultaneous EEG + ECG).

### Prediction 3: ρ_cross decreases with age faster than individual A values

The cross-system coherence of asymmetry (ρ between A_EEG and A_HRV) degrades faster than the asymmetry of either system alone. This means: the organism loses coordination between systems before either system individually fails. This is the most original prediction and would constitute a novel aging biomarker.

### Prediction 4: Inter-scale transfer entropy shifts in pathology

- Healthy: TE(fast→slow) ≈ TE(slow→fast) — balanced coupling
- Epilepsy: TE(fast→slow) >> TE(slow→fast) — upward breach
- Coma: TE(slow→fast) >> TE(fast→slow) — downward dominance
- Aging: both TE values decrease — decoupling

### Prediction 5: Tonic stimulation reduces A(τ) and ρ_cross

Pharmacological or pathological conversion of pulsatile → tonic signaling should measurably reduce A(τ) and cross-system coherence. Example: chronic benzodiazepine use (tonic GABA enhancement) should reduce EEG asymmetry at gamma/beta scales.

### Prediction 6: A(τ) at cellular level correlates with senescence markers

In calcium imaging data, senescent cells should show lower A(τ) (flatter Ca²⁺ transients, slower rise, relatively faster decay) compared to young cells. This bridges the molecular and organismal scales through the same formalism.

## 7. Verification Plan

### Phase 1: Core Hypothesis — LEMON Dataset (1-2 months)

**Dataset:** LEMON (Leipzig Study for Mind-Body-Emotion Interactions), 227 subjects, simultaneous 62-channel EEG + ECG, age range 20-77.

**Pipeline:**

1. **Preprocessing EEG:**
   - Standard artifact rejection (ICA-based)
   - CRITICAL: cardiac artifact removal (identified as a confounder by 2025 eLife paper linking age-related EEG changes to cardiac activity). Use ICA components correlated with ECG to remove cardiac contamination before computing EEG asymmetry.
   - Wavelet decomposition into 6 scales: delta (1-4 Hz), theta (4-8 Hz), alpha (8-13 Hz), beta (13-30 Hz), low gamma (30-50 Hz), high gamma (50-80 Hz).

2. **Preprocessing ECG:**
   - R-peak detection → RR intervals → HRV time series
   - Wavelet decomposition into 4 scales: VLF (0.003-0.04 Hz), LF (0.04-0.15 Hz), HF (0.15-0.4 Hz), VHF (0.4-1.0 Hz)

3. **Compute A(τ) for each scale:**
   - For each oscillatory cycle at each scale: measure t_rise (trough to peak) and t_fall (peak to trough)
   - A(τ) = median across all cycles of (t_fall − t_rise)/(t_fall + t_rise)
   - Output per subject: A_EEG(τ) profile (6 values) and A_HRV(τ) profile (4 values)

4. **Test Prediction 1:** One-sample t-test: A(τ) > 0 at each scale.

5. **Test Prediction 2:** Linear regression: A(τ) ~ age, at each scale. Expected: negative slope (A decreases with age).

6. **Test Prediction 3:** 
   - Compute ρ_cross = correlation between A_EEG and A_HRV profiles (interpolating to common scale axis if needed)
   - Linear regression: ρ_cross ~ age. Expected: negative slope.
   - Compare effect sizes: |β_ρcross| > |β_A_individual|

7. **Test Prediction 4:**
   - Transfer entropy between wavelet scales within EEG, within HRV, and between EEG and HRV
   - TE ~ age regression for each direction

**Expected output:** A paper with title approximately: "Asymmetric oscillatory waveforms across scales as a cross-system biomarker of aging."

### Phase 2: Replication and Extension (1-2 months, parallel)

**Dataset:** PhysioNet/Fantasia (40 subjects, 20 young + 20 old, simultaneous ECG + respiration). Different cohort, different equipment.

**Additional dataset:** MESA or SHHS from NSRR (thousands of subjects, sleep polysomnography with EEG + ECG + respiratory channels).

**Extension:** Add respiratory signal as third organ system. Compute A_resp(τ) and triple cross-coherence. Prediction: adding a third system increases sensitivity to aging effects.

### Phase 3: Cellular Scale (2-3 months)

**Dataset:** Calcium imaging datasets (MAESTRO platform outputs, or published GCaMP time-lapse from Dryad/Zenodo).

**Analysis:**
- Compute A(τ) for Ca²⁺ oscillations in individual cells
- Compare A between: young vs. old cells; stressed vs. unstressed; proliferating vs. senescent (SA-β-gal positive)
- If possible: compare with simultaneously measured proliferation rate, mitochondrial membrane potential

**Expected output:** Demonstration that A(τ) > 0 at cellular scale and A decreases in senescent/stressed cells.

### Phase 4: Meta-Analytic Scale Map (parallel with Phases 1-3)

**Method:** Systematic literature review. For each biological oscillator from molecular to organismal scale, extract published values of t_rise and t_fall (or waveform asymmetry metrics).

**Sources:**
- Ion channels: single-channel patch-clamp literature (activation/inactivation kinetics)
- Ca²⁺: published calcium transient waveforms
- Action potentials: Allen Brain Observatory, published electrophysiology
- ECG: QRS width vs. T-wave duration (trivially asymmetric; quantify across ages)
- Respiration: inspiratory vs. expiratory time
- HRV: acceleration vs. deceleration runs
- Endocrine: pulse rise vs. fall times (GnRH, insulin, cortisol)
- Circadian: cortisol awakening response (rise) vs. evening decline (fall)
- Immune: onset vs. resolution of acute inflammation
- Wound healing: injury response vs. remodeling phase

**Output:** A(τ) plotted as a function of τ from nanoseconds to years. If A is approximately constant or monotonically varying across 15 orders of magnitude of timescale — this is a publishable result on its own, regardless of the LEMON analysis outcome.

### Phase 5: Pathology Contrast (3-6 months)

**Datasets:**
- Epilepsy: Temple University Hospital EEG Corpus (TUH) — thousands of EEG recordings with seizure annotations. Prediction: A(τ) at high-frequency scales inverts or collapses during seizure; inter-scale TE becomes unidirectional (fast→slow).
- Heart failure: PhysioNet CHFDB. Prediction: HRV asymmetry collapses.
- Depression: publicly available EEG datasets with clinical scores. Prediction: reduced A at slow scales.
- Cancer: time-lapse imaging of tumor spheroids/organoids (emerging datasets). Prediction: A(τ) at tissue scale is reduced or inverted.

## 8. What Makes This Novel

### 8.1 Individual tools exist; the framework is new

- Spectral exponent β: widely measured
- EEG waveform asymmetry (sawtooth-ness): measured in some studies
- HRV acceleration/deceleration asymmetry: measured
- Transfer entropy between brain and heart: measured
- Phase-amplitude coupling across scales: measured

**Nobody has:**
1. Interpreted oscillatory waveform asymmetry as the fundamental signature of living perception cycles
2. Measured coherence of this asymmetry across organ systems as a biomarker
3. Connected inter-scale information flow to the "nested recovery" principle
4. Provided a unified framework predicting that the same quantity A(τ) should be positive from ion channels to circadian rhythms, and should decrease coherently with aging and disease

### 8.2 The framework makes predictions that existing frameworks do not

- That cross-system A coherence degrades faster than individual A (Prediction 3) — no existing biomarker framework predicts this
- That tonic pharmacological stimulation should reduce A(τ) (Prediction 5) — testable and specific
- That cellular A predicts senescence (Prediction 6) — bridges molecular and systems biology through one metric

### 8.3 Philosophical depth

The framework grounds biophysics in a principle about the relationship between living systems and time: **living matter is matter for which the path through state space matters, not just the endpoints.** The asymmetry of the perception cycle is the minimal signature of this path-dependence. A non-living system has A = 0 on all scales (symmetric response). A living system has A > 0 — and the magnitude and cross-scale coherence of A is a measure of how alive the system is.

This connects to:
- Free Energy Principle (Friston): active inference requires asymmetric cycling (sense → act → recover → sense)
- Criticality / 1/f noise: β ≈ 1 as the spectral signature of balanced inter-scale coupling
- Autopoiesis: self-production requires asymmetric metabolic cycling
- Anokhin's functional systems: goal-directed behavior as nested anticipatory cycles

## 9. Risks and Mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation |
|------|-----------|
| Cardiac artifacts in EEG contaminate A_EEG | ICA cleaning with explicit ECG reference; report results with and without artifact removal |
| A(τ) > 0 may be trivially true for any nonlinear oscillator | Test on surrogate data (phase-randomized signals preserve power spectrum but destroy asymmetry); show that A decreases with shuffling |
| Cross-coherence ρ may be driven by shared autonomic modulation | Partial correlation controlling for respiration rate and sympathovagal balance |
| Insufficient age range in LEMON | Supplement with MESA/SHHS (wider age range, N > 1000) |
| A(τ) metric too noisy for single-subject assessment | Report group-level statistics first; optimize metric (median vs. mean, winsorization, bootstrap CI) |
